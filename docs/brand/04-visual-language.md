# 04 — Visual Language

**Version 3 · foundation document 4 of 5**
**Date:** 4 August 2026
**Status:** Draft for approval
**Governed by:** `01-vision.md`, `02-positioning.md`, `03-design-principles.md`

This document explains how the studio sees. It contains no values. Typefaces, palettes, scales, curves and components are decided in the design phase and recorded there — and every one of those decisions has to be defensible from a line in here.

Nothing in this document should need rewriting because the interface changed.

---

## 1. What the visual system is for

The system is a **room**, not a picture. Its job is to hold material it did not create — a photographer's images, an artist's work, a couple's afternoon — without imposing a house look on any of it.

Two layers, and the boundary between them is the most important decision in the system:

| Layer | What it is | Changes per project |
|---|---|---|
| **Studio layer** | The room. Structure, sequence, and the behaviour of type, space, light and movement. | No |
| **Project layer** | Everything the material brings. Imagery, colour, sound, texture, pacing. | Entirely |

The boundary has one test: **would this still be right if the material were replaced tomorrow?** If yes, it belongs to the studio layer. If it would look wrong beside a different client's work, it was never structure — it was a decoration we had grown used to.

The layers are not equal. The studio layer exists to be underneath. When the two disagree, the material wins, because the material is the reason anyone came.

→ *If the studio layer is recognisable across projects, it is too loud. We are recognised by how the work behaves, not by how it looks.*

## 2. Light is the primary variable

The system's main axis is not colour. It is **light level** — how lit the room is at this point in the story.

Light is the one visual variable everybody already reads without being taught. People know what late evening means, and what a plain afternoon means, before they know anything about us. It carries time, weather, intimacy and attention at no cost, and it belongs to no trend, which is why it can be the foundation.

So grounds are narrative states, not themes. Dark is *before* and *inside*. Light is *present* and *plain*. A change of ground is an event in the story, and it is used rarely enough to stay one.

→ *Every element exists twice: lit and unlit. One that only works on a single ground is unfinished — not adaptable.*
→ *A ground change is one event. While the light is changing, nothing else does.*

## 3. Colour

Colour belongs mostly to the project, not to us.

The studio's own colour does very little, on purpose. Its job is to leave the material as the most saturated thing present — a photograph, a painting, a room at dusk. An interface that also wants to be colourful enters a competition with the work it is holding, and it loses honestly: the work is better.

- Saturated colour arrives from the material. Never from the interface.
- Where the interface does use colour, it reports **state** — something is live, focused, selected, changed. It does not set mood. Mood comes from the material and from the light.
- Colour never carries meaning alone. Someone who cannot see the difference still receives the message.
- Colour used to make a screen feel expensive is decoration.

## 4. Typography

Type is the studio's voice, and on many screens it is the only thing present. When there is no image, no sound and no colour doing work, type carries the moment by itself. This is why it is a matter of philosophy and not only specification: almost everything else on a screen may belong to the client, but the setting of words is always ours.

Type does two jobs, and they pull against each other.

One is **to speak** — a few words that have to land, be remembered, and hold the weight of an entrance. The other is **to disappear** — a paragraph someone should finish without noticing they were reading. Nothing does both well, and a system that pretends otherwise produces statements that are merely legible and paragraphs that are exhausting. Recognising that these are two different jobs is the decision. How many faces answer it is not.

Reading is the floor, not the goal. Text has to be comfortable before it is allowed to be beautiful, and comfort is confirmed at a real size on a real screen at a real distance — never in a layout.

Type is set, not decorated. Its expression comes from size, weight, the space around it, and where the line ends. Not from effects applied to letters. In a statement, where the line breaks is part of the composition, which means somebody chose it.

**What this prevents:** words used as placeholder until the real copy arrives · a face chosen for a headline being asked to carry a paragraph · atmosphere bought at the cost of reading · type animated to make it interesting, when what is uninteresting is the sentence.

## 5. Composition

- **Margins are silence.** Emptiness is an instrument, not what is left over. It is the first thing anyone asks to reduce and the thing most worth defending.
- **Asymmetry by default.** An asymmetric composition has direction; it tells the eye where to go first. Centring stops the eye and holds it, so centring is reserved for moments meant to be ceremonial.
- **A frame is chosen, not inherited.** The shape of an image is a composition decision made for that moment and then held. An image is never reshaped to fit the space it lands in — where a moment needs a different shape, that is a different composition.
- **Scale is currency.** A large moment is only large beside restrained ones. If everything is big, nothing arrives.
- **A grid exists so that breaking it is legible.** Order is what makes a departure readable as a departure. Break it once, deliberately.

## 6. Imagery

The material is the subject, and the subject is someone else's. Our job is framing, sequence and restraint.

- **The subject is real.** Real places, real people, real occasions. Borrowed or simulated material fails before anyone judges it: it is not the client's, so it cannot be about them.
- **One art direction per project,** agreed before anything is made, applied to every asset — including the ones nobody planned for.
- **What is left out of a frame is part of the frame.** Suggestion asks the viewer to lean in. Showing everything asks nothing of them.
- **Moving image earns its motion.** If a still does the same work, it is the still. Movement that only demonstrates the page is capable of movement is decoration.
- **Sequence is composition.** The same two images in the other order are a different meaning.

## 7. Motion

Motion exists for one reason: to make change comprehensible. A person needs to know that something has arrived, that what they did was received, or that the room they are standing in has changed. Motion answers those three questions. It has no other job.

So motion is a property of the room, not an effect applied to it. Things move the way things move — with weight, in a direction, once.

| What has happened | What the motion says |
|---|---|
| **Something entered** | This is new and worth finding. Slow enough to be noticed, never fast enough to have to be caught. |
| **Someone acted** | You were heard. Immediate — the answer arrives before anyone wonders whether it will. |
| **The light changed** | The room is different now. The largest and slowest change the system makes. |

What follows from that:

- Motion is described by what it means, never by the name of an effect. "Something arrives" is a brief. "A parallax fade-up" is a decision taken before anyone asked what was happening.
- If nothing has changed in meaning, nothing moves.
- A beat happens once. Returning to a place is not an event, so nothing performs itself again because somebody came back.
- Interface motion resolves. Movement that never ends has finished communicating and is only asking for attention it cannot repay.

## 8. Sound

Sound is a design material, held to the strictest rule in the system: **it never starts on its own.** Someone asks for it, can always stop it, and is not asked again.

Everything the sound does, the silent version does too. Sound deepens the work; it never completes it. Most people will never hear it, and what they get has to be a version of the work rather than a reduced one.

## 9. Texture and material

Texture is permitted when it comes from the project's physical world — the paper the invitation was printed on, the wall the work hangs on, the grain of the film it was shot on. Then it is information about the subject.

→ *Texture added to make a screen feel expensive is decoration. Principle 4.*

## 10. Chrome

Navigation, controls and forms are where a studio most easily begins to look like every other studio, because they are the parts most easily borrowed.

Chrome is how the room is operated. It is not part of what the room is for. That sentence decides most of it.

- **Understood before it is used.** Nothing about moving through the work should have to be learned. A control that needs explaining is the wrong control, not an opportunity for a tooltip.
- **Present when wanted, quiet when not.** Chrome earns permanence by being needed continuously. Most of it is not, and should not behave as though it were.
- **It always answers.** Every input receives an immediate, visible acknowledgement. Silence after an action is the moment someone notices the mechanism — Principle 6 makes that the most expensive thing we can do.
- **Nothing is inherited.** Interface elements take their form from this system, not from whatever component vocabulary is current. Convention is not a reason. It is only evidence that something has been done a lot.
- **State is designed.** Focus, hover, pressed, disabled, error and empty are part of the visual language, decided alongside everything else — not defaults left in place, and not an accessibility pass at the end.
- **Forms are conversations.** Plain questions, in sentences, asking only for what will actually be used. Every additional field is something we made a person do for our own convenience.

## 11. Forbidden

Not a list of what is currently unfashionable. Fashion moves, that list would need rewriting, and needing to be rewritten is how you know it was never philosophy.

Four things are forbidden, each because it contradicts something above.

1. **Anything that draws attention to the mechanism.** Effects performed so that the effect is noticed. Transitions displaying their own cleverness. Letters arriving one at a time. The moment the machinery becomes visible, the person is outside the experience. Principle 6.
2. **Anything that holds a person in place.** Sequences that cannot be skipped, screens that exist to be waited through, moments that detain somebody in order to prove they were worth making. We decide the order. They decide the pace. Principle 2.
3. **Anything that argues for us.** Counting, awards, walls of logos, rotating praise. Claiming quality is what gets done instead of demonstrating it — and the work is on the same page, entirely capable of doing the demonstrating.
4. **Anything that would survive the material being removed.** If a screen still looks impressive with the subject taken out of it, that screen was about us. Principle 5.

Bans on specific techniques belong in the design system, where they can be revised when the technique stops appearing.

## 12. Language

Words are part of the sensory system, not a separate discipline, and they are set by the same rules as everything else here: plain, specific, unhurried. Short declarative sentences. Concrete nouns.

We describe what happened. We do not rate it. No superlatives — a superlative is a claim the reader has to take on trust, and the work is right there to be looked at instead.

→ *If a sentence would survive being moved to another studio's site, it is not ours yet.*

That test lasts. A list of currently overused words does not: today's is *crafted, bespoke, seamless, immersive, curated, elevate, unforgettable, passionate, journey, solutions*, it is banned from all copy, and it will need replacing — which is why the test sits above it.
